## The tool that cut faster than steel
images: lathe, shop, toolbit

At the start of the twentieth century, every machine shop in the world had the same problem. Steel was getting harder. Machines were getting stronger. Electric motors and heavier lathes could push a cutting tool faster than ever before. But the tool itself kept failing. Push it too fast and the edge began to glow, then it softened, and within minutes it was gone. The machinist stopped the lathe, walked to the grinder, and started again.

Then an inventor in a small town in Indiana made something strange. It was a metal with almost no iron in it at all. It did not rust. It did not tarnish. And when it was heated to a dull red, the temperature at which ordinary tool steel turns soft as butter, it stayed hard.

He named it after the stars.

For a few decades this alloy was one of the most important cutting materials in industry. It helped feed the factories of the First World War. It went into the engines that crossed the Atlantic. And then, almost as quickly as it arrived, it was pushed aside by something harder.

Today almost nobody outside a machine shop or a welding supply store has heard of it. But it never really went away. So what happened to Stellite?

## A machinist's problem
images: lathe, shop, toolbit

To understand why Stellite mattered, we have to start with the oldest problem in machining.

Cutting metal is a strange act. A lathe tool does not slice through steel the way a knife cuts bread. It pushes into the material and forces a layer of it to shear away as a chip. That chip slides across the top of the tool under enormous pressure. The friction and the deformation turn almost all of that work into heat, and most of that heat gathers in a tiny zone right at the cutting edge.

For most of the nineteenth century, tools were made of carbon steel. Carbon steel can be made very hard by heating it and quenching it in water or oil. But that hardness is borrowed. Heat the tool again, even to a few hundred degrees, and the hardness starts to drain away. The steel tempers itself while it works. So the machinist had a simple rule: go slow, keep it cool, and never let the edge change color.

That rule set the speed of the entire industrial world. It limited how fast a crankshaft could be turned, how fast a gun barrel could be bored, and how many parts a factory could make in a day.

## The first escape: tungsten steel
images: mushet, taylor, paris

The first escape came from England. In 1868 a metallurgist named Robert Forester Mushet developed a steel with tungsten and manganese in it. Mushet steel hardened simply by cooling in air, and it held its edge longer than carbon steel. It was a real step forward, though it was expensive and brittle, and most shops used it only for the hardest jobs.

The bigger leap came at the end of the century, in Pennsylvania. In 1899 and 1900, Frederick Winslow Taylor and Maunsel White, working at the Bethlehem Steel Company, experimented with heating tungsten tool steels to temperatures that everyone else considered ruinous. The result was a steel that kept cutting even when its edge ran hot. Cutting speeds roughly tripled.

At the Paris Exposition of 1900, tools made from the new steel were shown cutting at speeds that seemed impossible, with the edge running hot and still cutting. The demonstration caused a sensation. This was the birth of what we now call high-speed steel.

High-speed steel changed machining almost overnight. But it was still steel. It still depended on heat treatment for its hardness, and it still had a limit. Push it hard enough and it, too, would give up. The door was open for something different.

## Elwood Haynes of Kokomo
images: haynes, pioneer, kokomo

The man who walked through that door was not a steelmaker at all.

Elwood Haynes was born in Portland, Indiana, in 1857. He trained as a teacher and studied chemistry, then worked in the natural gas industry that boomed across Indiana in the 1880s. He settled in the town of Kokomo, and there he became famous for something else entirely. On July the fourth, 1894, Haynes test drove one of the first gasoline automobiles built in the United States. He called it the Pioneer.

But cars were only part of his story. Haynes was obsessed with metals. Around 1895 he began searching for a new hard alloy that would resist rusting, something he could use for engine parts and, later, for cutlery and instruments. Iron was the obvious starting point for almost every metallurgist of the time. Haynes went in another direction. He began experimenting with cobalt and chromium, two metals that most of the steel industry barely used.

What he found was remarkable. An alloy of cobalt and chromium, with no iron to speak of, took a brilliant polish and kept it. It did not stain. It did not tarnish in air or in many acids. In 1907 Haynes received patents on two binary alloys: one of nickel and chromium, and one of cobalt and chromium.

He chose a name from the Latin word stella, meaning star, because of the bright, lasting shine of the metal's surface. He called it Stellite.

## Adding tungsten
images: toolbit, lathe, haynes

At first Haynes thought of Stellite as a material for table knives, surgical and dental instruments and spark plug parts. But he kept experimenting. He added tungsten to the cobalt and chromium, and the alloy changed character. It became much harder, hard enough to cut other metals.

In 1913 Haynes was granted a patent on this three-part alloy of cobalt, chromium and tungsten. The patent described a range of compositions, with tungsten anywhere from five to sixty percent. Haynes recommended a mix with roughly a quarter to a half tungsten and around fifteen percent chromium, with cobalt making up the rest.

The courts later examined this alloy in a patent dispute, and their description is worth repeating. They noted that ordinary tool steel held its edge fairly well up to about six hundred degrees Celsius. Haynes's alloy kept its hardness from around six hundred all the way up to about one thousand degrees. The court called this red hardness entirely novel among commercial alloys, and stated that in suitable work it allowed double the cutting speed of the best high-speed steel.

That was the secret of Stellite. It was not a steel at all. It was not hardened by quenching. It was hard as it came out of the mold. There was no temper to lose, so when the edge glowed, nothing inside the metal changed. Stellite tools could be cast directly to shape and ground to a cutting edge, and they kept on cutting where steel would fail.

## Red hardness, explained
images: lathe, toolbit, shop

Red hardness is worth a moment of its own, because it is the whole story of cutting tool materials in two words.

Every cutting tool material is a compromise between hardness and toughness. Hardness lets the edge resist wear. Toughness lets it survive shocks without chipping. But at the cutting edge there is a third enemy, and that is temperature. A tool can be hard on the bench and soft in the cut.

Carbon steel loses its hardness at a few hundred degrees. High-speed steel pushed that limit much higher. Stellite pushed it higher still, and it did so in a completely different way. In steel, hardness comes from a structure trapped by quenching, and heat lets that structure relax. In Stellite, hardness comes from hard carbides of chromium and tungsten locked inside a tough cobalt matrix. Heat does not undo that structure. The alloy is the same at nine hundred degrees as it was at room temperature, just a little softer.

Every cutting material that came after Stellite would follow the same idea: hard particles held together by a tougher metal. Keep that in mind, because it is about to come back.

## The factory and the war
images: haynes, kokomo, munitions

In 1912 Haynes formed the Haynes Stellite Works on Union Street in Kokomo. In 1915, together with two local businessmen, he incorporated it as the Haynes Stellite Company.

The timing could not have been better. The First World War created a demand for machined metal on a scale that no one had planned for. Shells, gun barrels, engine parts and aircraft components all had to be turned, bored and faced, by the million. Every minute a tool spent cutting instead of being reground meant more output. Stellite tools, able to run hot and fast on tough material, became a serious industrial product.

The company received lucrative wartime contracts. By 1916, Elwood Haynes was a millionaire.

In 1920 Haynes sold the company to Union Carbide, which was building a large business in alloys and industrial gases. Haynes himself died in Kokomo in 1925, at the age of sixty-seven. He is also remembered as one of the independent discoverers of stainless steel, an alloy he worked out around the same time as the English metallurgist Harry Brearley. It is a striking record for one man in one small town: an early car, a family of cobalt alloys, and a share in stainless steel.

## Hardfacing and the Atlantic crossing
images: hardfacing, valve, spirit

Under Union Carbide, Stellite found a second career that would outlast the first.

In the early 1920s the company developed a hardfacing process: welding a layer of Stellite onto the surface of a part made of ordinary steel. Instead of making the whole part from expensive cobalt alloy, you could put a thin, hard skin exactly where wear happened.

One of the first great uses was in engine valves. A poppet valve in an engine slams shut against its seat thousands of times a minute, in hot, corrosive exhaust gas. A Stellite face on the valve and the seat made them last far longer.

In 1927 Charles Lindbergh flew the Spirit of St. Louis from New York to Paris, alone, nonstop, across the Atlantic. The engine that carried him included hard surfaced valves made by Haynes. The cobalt alloy invented for cutlery in a small Indiana town was now inside one of the most famous flights in history.

## The material that replaced it
images: krupp, carbide, insert

Back in the machine shop, though, a new material was coming.

In Germany, researchers at the lamp maker Osram had been working with tungsten for light bulb filaments. Tungsten carbide, a compound of tungsten and carbon, was known to be extraordinarily hard, but on its own it was far too brittle to use. In 1923 Karl Schröter, head of research at Osram, filed a patent on a way around the problem. He pressed fine tungsten carbide powder together with a small amount of cobalt and sintered it into a solid body. The cobalt melted around the carbide grains and held them together like cement.

At the end of 1925 Osram sold the license to Krupp. In 1926 Krupp brought the material to market under the name Widia, from the German wie Diamant, like diamond. It was shown at the Leipzig fair in 1927, and by the late 1920s cemented carbide was reaching shops in America as well.

The early carbide tips were brittle and tricky to use. They chipped if the machine vibrated, and they had to be brazed onto steel shanks by hand. But they were far harder than Stellite, and as machines grew more rigid they could run at speeds no cast alloy could match. Reports from the time describe steel being cut at up to one hundred and fifty meters per minute, nearly four times faster than high-speed steel.

Over the following decades, brazed carbide tips gave way to indexable carbide inserts, and then to coated inserts. Carbide took over the cutting edge.

Now notice the irony. Cemented carbide is made of hard particles held in a tough metal binder, exactly the idea that made Stellite work. And the binder that holds most carbide together is cobalt, the metal at the heart of Stellite. The material that replaced Stellite was built from its own core ingredient.

## Why Stellite lost the cutting race
images: insert, lathe, toolbit

Stellite did not lose because it was bad. It lost because carbide was better at the one thing that mattered most in metal cutting: hardness at speed.

A cast cobalt alloy sits in an awkward middle position. It is tougher than carbide and better at high temperature than high-speed steel. For a while, that middle position was valuable. Cast alloy tools were used for heavy cuts on rough castings and for jobs where carbide would chip. But every improvement in carbide, tougher grades, better geometry, better machines, squeezed that middle space smaller.

There was also the question of cost. Cobalt is expensive, and a solid cast tool uses a lot of it. A carbide insert uses only a small amount of cobalt as a binder, and when one edge wears out, you simply rotate it to a fresh one. For a factory counting every cent per part, the choice became obvious.

By the second half of the twentieth century, cast cobalt alloys had largely disappeared from the lathe. As cutting tools, they had become a footnote.

## Where Stellite survives today
images: saw, hardfacing, dental

And yet Stellite did not disappear. It moved to places where wear, heat and corrosion all attack at once, and where toughness matters as much as hardness.

It is welded onto the teeth of sawmill blades, where it lets a saw cut through wood full of grit and sap for far longer before sharpening. It still protects valve seats and valves in engines and in power plants. It is used on pump impellers, wear rings and shafts that grind against abrasive fluids. The first section of the barrel in some heavy machine guns is lined with a Stellite alloy to resist the erosion of hot propellant gas.

It even found its way into medicine. The cage of the Starr-Edwards heart valve, one of the first commercially available artificial heart valves, first implanted in 1960, was made from a Stellite alloy. Cobalt chromium alloys are still used for dental prostheses today.

The business changed hands many times. Union Carbide sold the company to the Cabot Corporation in 1970. In 1985 Cabot sold the Stellite hardfacing business, and the rest of the Kokomo operation became Haynes International, a maker of high-performance nickel and cobalt alloys. The Stellite trademark itself was acquired by Kennametal in 2012, a company that grew up making, of all things, carbide cutting tools.

## What it teaches
images: toolbit, shop, lathe

Stellite lost the race it became famous for, but it never lost its edge.

Its story is the story of almost every cutting material. A breakthrough. A golden era. A faster rival. And then a second life somewhere nobody expected. Carbon steel gave way to tungsten steel. Tungsten steel gave way to high-speed steel. High-speed steel and Stellite gave way to carbide. And carbide itself now shares the edge with ceramics, cubic boron nitride and diamond.

What stays the same is the problem. A small, hot, punishing zone at the tip of a tool, where metal meets metal and the material that survives the heat wins.

Elwood Haynes went looking for a metal that would not tarnish on a dinner table. He found one that changed how the world cut steel, flew across the Atlantic inside a famous engine, and still guards saw teeth and valve seats more than a century later.

That is what happened to Stellite.
